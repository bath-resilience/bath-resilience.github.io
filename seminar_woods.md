## Abstract

Melting snow is a key source of water for more than 2 billion people. Changes in climate are already causing changes in the amount and timing of snowmelt, and substantial further changes are expected in future. Communicating why these changes are happening, and why they aren't the same everywhere, will help water managers to navigate these changes. At present, we have scientific capability to calculate the expected changes in snow, but not to explain conceptually why the effects vary from place to place. The difficulty arises because snowpack dynamics are driven by nonlinear (but elementary) interactions between weather elements such as temperature and precipitation, at multiple timescales. In this talk I will (i) show some typical snow and climate data (ii) present a deterministic snowpack model and explain its strengths and weaknesses (iii) propose a non-stationary stochastic model for the snowpack, and show some preliminary results.
